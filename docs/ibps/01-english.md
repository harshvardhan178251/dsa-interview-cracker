# IBPS PO — English Language

**Prelims:** 30 questions · 30 marks · 20 minutes
**Mains:** 40 questions · 20 marks · 35 minutes
**Level:** basic → advanced

English is the section where preparation pays back fastest. It has the least maths, the
highest floor, and in Mains it is worth only **half a mark per question** — the cheapest
scoring in the paper.

## Layer 1 — the seven grammar rules that cover most error spotting

Almost every error-spotting question is one of these. Learn them as **checks you run in
order**, not as a list you hope to recognise.

### 1. Subject–verb agreement

The verb must match the subject in **number**, and the subject is what it is — not what
the phrase around it suggests.

> ✗ *The number of applicants **are** increasing.*
> ✓ *The number of applicants **is** increasing.*

The trap: `number of` takes a singular verb, `a number of` takes a plural one.

> ✗ *A number of employees **has** resigned.*
> ✓ *A number of employees **have** resigned.*

Also watch the intervening phrase:

> ✗ *The quality of the mangoes **are** poor.* → *is* poor
> ✓ *The reasons for the delay **are** clear.*

**Test:** strip the subject down to its head noun and count.

### 2. Tense consistency

One passage, one tense — unless the sentence explicitly changes it ("but then…", "later",
"eventually").

> ✗ *He walked to the station and **takes** the train.*
> ✓ *He walked to the station and **took** the train.*

**Watch the sequence:** past perfect for the earlier of two past events.

> ✓ *By the time the auditor arrived, the accounts **had been** tampered with.*

### 3. The article

`a` / `an` = first mention, countable singular. `the` = already identified, or unique.

- ✗ *He is **a** honest man.* → *an* honest
- ✗ *She plays **the** cricket well.* → no article
- ✗ *The life is difficult.* → *Life* (generalisation, no article)

**The "the" test:** if you can put "a/an" in front of it and still make sense, it usually
takes no article.

### 4. Prepositions of time and place

| Use | Preposition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| At a point in time | **at** | *at 5 o'clock, at midnight, at the weekend* |
| On a day or date | **on** | *on Monday, on 5 March* |
| In a period | **in** | *in April, in the morning, in 2026* |
| At a place | **at** | *at the station, at home* |
| In a larger place | **in** | *in Delhi, in India* |
| On a surface | **on** | *on the table, on the wall* |

**The classic trap:** *in* vs *on* for days — "on Monday", but "in the morning".

### 5. The dangling modifier

A participial phrase must modify the subject of the main clause. If it attaches to
something else, it dangles.

> ✗ *Walking through the market, the rain soaked my bag.*
> ✓ *Walking through the market, **I** was soaked by rain.*

**The test:** mentally re-attach the phrase. "Walking through the market" must describe
the subject. If the subject is "the rain", it is dangling.

### 6. Pronoun agreement and reference

A pronoun must match the noun it replaces in **number and gender**, and it must refer to
something already mentioned.

> ✗ *The company told the employees that **they** will get a bonus.* — *they* = employees ✓ but if *they* = the company, it is wrong. Check which antecedent is grammatical.

> ✗ *Ravi asked about **his** results.* — ambiguous only if two people are present. Look for a real antecedent.

### 7. Redundancy and the verb

**Redundancy:** the subject and verb say the same thing.

> ✗ *He **returned back** to office.*
> ✗ *She **reverted back** to the original plan.*
> ✗ *The **final outcome** was unexpected.* — *outcome* already means final.

**Common redundancies:** return back, revert back, repeat again, new innovation, past
history, final conclusion, free gift, end result, future plans, advance planning,
cancel cancellation.

## Layer 2 — reading comprehension, done properly

RC is 8–10 questions in Prelims and 10–17 in Mains. It is the single largest block of
marks in the section, and the questions are graded **all-or-nothing per passage**: you
either understand the passage or you do not.

### The method: four passes, not one

**Pass 1 — skim (30 seconds).**
Read the first paragraph, the first line of every paragraph, and the last paragraph.
Write down, in three words: *what is this about?*

**Pass 2 — the questions (60 seconds).**
Read the questions **before** the passage. Underline what each question asks for:
- *Who / What / When* → factual
- *Why / How* → inference
- *Which of the following* → main idea, tone, or an author's opinion
- *Fill in the blanks* → vocabulary in context

**Pass 3 — read and hunt (the actual pass).**
Read the passage properly now, hunting only for the answers you marked. Underline the
answer line as you find it.

**Pass 4 — review (30 seconds).**
Check the options against the passage. Most wrong answers are *plausible but not
supported*. The passage is the only authority.

### The four question types and their traps

| Type | The trap |
|---|---|
| **Factual** | A detail that is true but not what was asked ("Which of the following is true?" vs "…is stated in the passage?") |
| **Inference** | You need a step of reasoning, but the conclusion must be **necessarily true**. If a better option exists, the inference is wrong. |
| **Main idea / Title** | Choose the option that **covers the whole passage**, not the most interesting part of it |
| **Tone / Author's view** | Match the tone word to the passage's mood: *neutral, critical, appreciative, sarcastic*. Find the author's attitude, not the topic. |

**The inference rule:** a valid inference is something that **must** be true given the
passage. "The author suggests" is only valid if the passage says so; if the passage
merely allows it, the answer is usually a trap.

**The main-idea rule:** the answer must be true of the **entire** passage. If one part of
the passage contradicts it, it is too narrow.

### RC passages to practise on

The exam's RC themes are predictable. Practise on:

- **Economy and banking** — RBI policy, inflation, digital banking
- **Social issues** — education, health, urbanisation, environment
- **Science and technology** — AI, space, climate
- **Editorials and opinion columns** — *The Hindu* editorial, *Indian Express* explainer

Read the editorial, then answer the *actual* questions that appeared with it on the
paper. That is free practice with the exact distribution.

## Layer 3 — cloze test

A cloze passage has blanks; you pick the word for each from four options.

**The method that works:**

1. **Read the whole passage first without filling anything.** Do not stop at each blank.
2. **Mark the "grammar blanks"** — the ones decided by structure, not meaning: articles,
   prepositions, subject–verb agreement, tenses, relative pronouns.
3. **Fill the grammar blanks first.** They are free marks and they narrow the options for
   the rest.
4. **Then use meaning and collocation** for the remaining blanks: does the word fit the
   sense, and does it collocate with its neighbours?

**Common collocation traps:**

| Correct | Wrong | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *make a decision* | *do a decision* | fixed verb |
| *raise a question* | *rise a question* | "rise" is intransitive |
| *strong emphasis* | *strong stress* | "emphasis" is idiomatic |
| *take a decision* | *make a decision* | both work; context decides |

**Eliminate on grammar before meaning.** In a cloze, most wrong options fail on grammar
first and only then on meaning.

## Layer 4 — para jumbles

You are given four or five sentences and must order them.

**The method:**

1. **Find the sentence with no pronoun and no reference to anything unmentioned.** That
   is almost always the **first** sentence — it introduces the topic.
2. **Find the closing sentence.** It is usually the one with a summary tone ("Thus",
   "In conclusion", a general statement) or the one referring to the whole discussion.
3. **Chain the rest** by pronouns: *this, these, such, it, they, he, also, however*.
4. **Check the connective flow**: "First… Then… Finally…", "Moreover", "Therefore".

**The two anchors solve it most of the time.** If you can only find one anchor, use it:
an opening sentence fixes positions 1 and the rest follow.

**Common trap:** a sentence containing *it* or *they* is rarely first, and a sentence
containing *thus / therefore / in short* is rarely anything but last.

## Layer 5 — vocabulary that pays

**Do not memorise a dictionary.** In the exam, vocabulary questions are usually
**vocabulary in context** — the word's meaning is inferable from the passage, and the
options are near-synonyms.

**The method:** substitute each option into the sentence and see which one makes the
sentence mean what the passage says. The right answer is usually the one that is
*more general* or *more precise* in the right direction.

**High-frequency word pairs worth knowing:**

| Word | Synonym | Antonym |
|---|---|---|
| Alleviate | relieve, mitigate | aggravate, worsen |
| Amenable | willing, compliant | resistant, obstinate |
| Candour | frankness, openness | cunning, evasion |
| Debacle | fiasco, disaster | triumph, success |
| Exacerbate | worsen, intensify | alleviate, ease |
| Frugal | thrifty, economical | extravagant, wasteful |
| Gregarious | sociable, outgoing | solitary, aloof |
| Lucrative | profitable, well-paid | unprofitable |
| Obscure | unclear, little-known | clear, famous |
| Pragmatic | practical, realistic | idealistic, dogmatic |
| Reticent | reserved, reluctant | forthcoming, talkative |
| Taciturn | quiet, reserved | talkative, garrulous |
| Vindicate | justify, exonerate | condemn, blame |

**Word-formation is tested too:** *reluctant → reluctance*, *mitigate → mitigation*,
*beneficial → beneficiary*. Learn the noun/adjective/adverb/verb forms together.

## Layer 6 — the descriptive paper (25 marks)

**Two questions: one essay, one letter (or one comprehension).** 30 minutes total —
about 15 minutes each.

**What the automated scoring looks at:** structure, clarity, grammar, vocabulary,
relevance, and whether the content addresses the prompt. It is not a human reading your
mind; it is a rubric. **Structure is worth the most and is the easiest to secure.**

**Essay — the 5-paragraph skeleton that always works:**

```
1. Introduction      — reframe the topic in your own words; state your position
2. Body 1            — argument 1, with a concrete example or data point
3. Body 2            — argument 2, with a counterpoint acknowledged
4. Body 3            — the counter-argument and your rebuttal, or a practical implication
5. Conclusion        — restate the position; one forward-looking sentence
```

**Letter — the 7-line format:**

```
1. Your address (right-aligned)
2. Date
3. "To," / "Dear Sir/Madam,"
4. Subject (underlined)
5. Body — 3 short paragraphs: why you are writing, the substance, the request
6. "Yours faithfully," (formal) / "Yours sincerely," (if you know the name)
7. Your name
```

**Topic themes (broadly based on):** economic and social issues, emerging trends in
banking and technology, current events, and ethics.

**The highest-scoring habits:**
- Write **at least** the essay. A blank essay is 0. A mediocre structured essay is 12+.
- **Plan for 3 minutes, write for 12.** A plan beats a longer answer.
- One concrete example per body paragraph — a scheme name, a statistic, a recent event.
  Specificity is what separates 12 from 18.
- Keep sentences short. Long sentences lose marks with automated scoring.
- No spelling mistakes in the first line — many rubrics weight it heavily.

## Exam traps by topic

| Topic | Trap |
|---|---|
| Error spotting | The sentence is **almost** right — pick the *least* wrong option |
| RC inference | A plausible inference that is not **necessarily** true |
| RC main idea | An option true of only one paragraph |
| Cloze | Choosing on meaning before eliminating on grammar |
| Para jumble | Anchoring on a pronoun sentence instead of the topic-introducing one |
| Vocabulary | Picking the more obscure word rather than the correct one |
| Sentence improvement | Assuming the "fancy" rewrite is better — sometimes the original is correct |

## Practice plan

| Weeks | What to do |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | Learn the seven grammar rules. Do 20 error-spotting questions a day. |
| 3–4 | RC: 2 passages a day, timed at 8 minutes each. Read editorials daily. |
| 5–6 | Cloze and para jumbles: 15 questions a day, then mixed sets of 30. |
| 7–8 | Vocabulary: build the word-pair list, 10 a day. |
| 9+ | Full Prelims mocks, twice a week, with a post-mock analysis of every wrong answer. |

**The analysis is the practice.** A mock you have not analysed was a mock you failed.
